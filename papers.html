  • Your proof
    Z-consistency, evidential decision theory, self-sampling, halfing and some thirding are incoherent – Bayes is doing just fine
    PDF (Draft 2) · Lean files
    From your present first-person perspective, the paper constructs a case and, from it, a synchronic proof in the standard probability calculus. A possible event that would settle your uncertainty about who, when or where you are, together with its mirror, partitions your measure into cells that give the event at issue the same probability. With no change of evidence, the construction proves a value the measure already has. The proof is given again for a case of everyday ignorance, with no loss of memory and no question of who or when you are. The same template proves Bayesian conditionalization from the axioms, so updating rules that diverge from it are disproved, Jeffrey's updating included (it is proved incoherent). Variants of the case show the views they target to be incoherent: compartmentalized conditioning, David Lewis's view and any view that leaves the half answer to Sleeping Beauty possible, some thirder views, all positions on one side of the absent-minded driver problem and some on the other, all self-sampling rules and with them the support for the Doomsday and simulation arguments, and evidential decision theory in Newcomb's problem. The consequences include Sleeping Beauty at 1/3, the planning optimum in the absent-minded driver, and two-boxing. Appendix A checks the main result independently of its major step, Appendix B gives a model in sets, and Appendix C writes the measure out in full.
  • Agreeing with myself
    Aumann's (1976) agreement theorem is proved here from a more elementary construction. Start with one algebra and one probability measure. Represent two cases that differ in which events have probability 0 by two cells of a partition of that measure, not by two measures and not by two stages of an updating process. Conditional probabilities are ratios already defined in the measure. If the cells give an event the same ratio, finite additivity gives the event that ratio unconditionally, whatever the cells' weights. The same single sum works for E and ¬E, and directly for any finite partition E₁, …, Eₙ, without induction. With the event definitions held fixed and the learned cell certain, the same construction gives Bayesian conditionalization at a later point, for every event at once. Conditionalization is then a calculation of a value the measure already has, not a rule for updating it. A second identity, the one-constant law, explains which ratios survive conditioning and why two cells agree on every ratio inside their overlap. Aumann's theorem follows by applying the finite-partition sum inside the common-knowledge event M, once for each agent's partition. Common knowledge supplies M and the constancy of the reported posteriors within it, and the remaining computation uses only one measure, ratios, additivity and positivity. The agents are then cells of one measure, and the agreement is agreement with oneself. Since Bayesian updating is a calculation, the result needs no common knowledge of rationality, on which Aumann's theorem implicitly depends, and no substantive assumptions that are not stated in the proof. What the theorem needs from learning is that what each agent conditions on is certain and true. The paper shows that in reasonable price-discovery situations these assumptions do not hold, and there a model can be given in which the theorem's conclusion fails: two people with a common prior and common knowledge veer apart. One measure cannot. Jeffrey's rule, which is order-dependent, would allow agreeing to disagree. But the rule is disproved, so it is no obstacle to the result.

  • Too clever by halfing
    with Tim Button and Daniel Rothschild · forthcoming in Oxford Studies in Epistemology
    We offer three arguments against the halfing response to the Sleeping Beauty problem. First, we outline an epistemological version of the sure-thing principle. Roughly: if it’s certain that the probability of E should be r, whichever of some distinct (pre-determined) options obtains, then the probability should be r, simpliciter. We show that double-halfers violate this principle. Second, we outline a principle of framing-invariance. Roughly: probability assignments should not depend upon mere artefacts of how we frame a scenario. We show that single-halfers violate this principle. Third, we show that any halfer who respects the framing-invariance principle must either give up their intuitions about “irrelevant” evidence or violate basic statistical reasoning.

  • Where is the Motivation in Motivated Numeracy?
    with Kathrin Glüer-Pagin · Review of Philosophy and Psychology 16(2): 481-498 (2025) · open access
    PDF (open access, typeset by the authors)publisher version
    In a series of very influential papers, Dan Kahan argues for “the identity protective cognition thesis”: the claim that politically motivated reasoning is a major factor explaining current levels of polarization over matters of fact, especially in the US. An important part of his case consists of experimental data supporting the claim that ideological polarization is more extreme amongst more numerate individuals. In this paper, we take a close look at how precisely this “numeracy effect” is supposed to come about. Working with Kahan’s own notion of motivated reasoning, we reconstruct the mechanism that according to him produces the effect. Surprisingly, it turns out to involve plenty of motivation to reason, but no motivated reasoning. This undermines the support he takes the numeracy effect to provide for the identity protective cognition hypothesis.

  • If you don’t know that you know, you could be surprised
    with Eli Pitcovski · Noûs 55(4): 917-934 (2021)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    Before the semester begins, a teacher tells his students: “There will be exactly one exam this semester. It will not take place on a day that is an immediate-successor of a day that you are currently in a position to know is not the exam-day”. Both the students and the teacher know – it is common knowledge – that no exam can be given on the first day of the semester. Since the teacher is truthful and reliable, it seems that the students can know that what he says is true. However, in that case, assuming the students can know that they know whatever it is they know (KK) and assuming their knowledge is closed under entailment (closure), the students can reason from what they know to the conclusion that no exam will take place during the semester. This conclusion contradicts what they supposedly know: that there will be an exam. This puzzle, we argue, gives rise to a new consideration for the rejection of KK. We discuss unique features of the argument, especially in comparison to Timothy Williamson’s rejection of KK in light of other versions of the surprise exam paradox.

  • Compartmentalized knowledge
    Philosophical Studies 176(10): 2785-2805 (2019)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    This paper explores some consequences of David Lewis’s (1996) understanding of how knowledge is compartmentalized. It argues, first, that he underestimates how badly it impacts his view. When knowledge is compartmentalized, it lacks at least one of two essential features of Lewis’s account: (a) Elusiveness—familiar skeptical possibilities, when relevant, are incompatible with everyday knowledge. (b) Knowledge is a modality—when a thinker knows that p, there is no relevant possibility where p is false. Lewis proposes compartmentalized knowledge to keep treating knowledge as a modality while mitigating one of its unrealistic epistemological implications: In normal modal epistemic logic (and standard possible world semantics), a thinker always counts as knowing the strongest proposition that follows from the set of all the individual propositions that this thinker knows. Lewis’s compartmentalization proposal is that thinkers merely know the conjunctions of propositions that are known in each of, but not across, their compartments. The irony is that in avoiding overblown knowledge the view now allows for thinkers to attend to skeptical error possibilities and yet knowledge is present. The account avoids inflation of knowledge in one sense only to acquire another type of knowledge the view denies subjects can have. This problem motivates an inspection of knowledge accounts whose intra-compartment closure principles are weaker than those that are valid in normal modal-logic. The conclusion is that some formulations of closure can avoid the challenge Lewis’s view faces. Nevertheless, even these closure principles pose a barrier—perhaps an implausible barrier—for knowledge of ignorance. Even when the reasoning supporting the lack of knowledge is sound, a subject cannot always know she doesn’t know. Interestingly, this obstacle is one that knowledge of knowledge doesn’t seem to face.
  • A pragmatic argument against equal weighting
    with Ittay Nissan-Rozen · Synthese 196(10): 4211-4227 (2019)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    We present a minimal pragmatic restriction on the interpretation of the weights in the “Equal Weight View” (and, more generally, in the “Linear Pooling” view) regarding peer disagreement and show that the view cannot respect it. Based on this result we argue against the view. The restriction is the following one: if an agent, i, assigns an equal or higher weight to another agent, j, (i.e. if i takes j to be as epistemically competent as him or epistemically superior to him), he must be willing – in exchange for a positive and certain payment – to accept an offer to let a completely rational and sympathetic j choose for him whether to accept a bet with positive expected utility. If i assigns a lower weight to j than to himself, he must not be willing to pay any positive price for letting j choose for him. Respecting the constraint entails, we show, that the impact of disagreement on one’s degree of belief is not independent of what the disagreement is discovered to be (i.e. not independent of j’s degree of belief).
  • At the Threshold of Knowledge
    with Daniel Rothschild · Philosophical Studies 175(2): 449-460 (2018)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    We explore consequences of the view that to know a proposition your rational credence in the proposition must exceed a certain threshold. In other words, to know something you must have evidence that makes rational a high credence in it. We relate such a threshold view to Dorr, Goodman, and Hawthorne’s (2014) argument against the principle they call fair coins: “If you know a coin won’t land tails, then you know it won’t be flipped.” They argue for rejecting fair coins because it leads to a pervasive skepticism about knowledge of the future. We argue that the threshold view of evidence and knowledge gives independent grounds to reject fair coins.
  • A Puzzle about Knowing Conditionals
    with Daniel Rothschild · Noûs 52(2): 473-478 (2018)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    We present a puzzle about knowledge, probability and conditionals. We show that in certain cases some basic and plausible principles governing our reasoning come into conflict. In particular, we show that there is a simple argument that a person may be in a position to know a conditional the consequent of which has a low probability conditional on its antecedent, contra Adams’ thesis. We suggest that the puzzle motivates a very strong restriction on the inference of a conditional from a disjunction.
  • Evidence and the Openness of Knowledge
    with Assaf Sharon · Philosophical Studies 174(4): 1001-1037 (2017)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    The paper argues that knowledge is not closed under logical inference. The argument proceeds from the openness of evidential support and the dependence of empirical knowledge on evidence, to the conclusion that knowledge is open. Without attempting to provide a full-fledged theory of evidence, we show that on the modest assumption that evidence cannot support both a proposition and its negation, or, alternatively, that information that reduces the probability of a proposition cannot constitute evidence for its truth, the relation of evidential support is not closed under known entailment. Therefore the evidence-for relation is deductively open regardless of whether evidence is probabilistic or not. Given even a weak dependence of empirical knowledge on evidence, we argue that empirical knowledge is also open. On this basis, we also respond to the strongest argument in support of knowledge closure (Hawthorne 2004a). Finally, we present a number of significant benefits of our position, namely, offering a unified explanation for a range of epistemological puzzles.

  • Mr. Magoo’s Mistake
    with Assaf Sharon · Philosophical Studies 139(2): 289-306 (2008)
    PDF (accepted manuscript)publisher version
    Timothy Williamson has famously argued that the (KK) principle (namely, that if one knows that p, then one knows that one knows that p) should be rejected. We analyze Williamson’s argument and show that its key premise is ambiguous, and that when it is properly stated this premise no longer supports the argument against (KK). After canvassing possible objections to our argument, we reflect upon some conclusions that suggest significant epistemological ramifications pertaining to the acquisition of knowledge from prior knowledge by deduction.
