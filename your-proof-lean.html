Your proof: the Lean files

Lean 4 check of the proofs in "Your proof"

Files (each stands alone)

  • YourProof.lean the main proof, Sure Thing, section 5.1, section 5.3, the models
  • Displays.lean the paper's other numbered displays, and numbers given in the text
  • OneBoxing.lean the skeleton of section 4.1
  • Cases.lean the cases of the paper as finite models, with their values
  • YourProof_any_field.lean YourProof.lean with values in any ordered field
  • Displays_any_field.lean Displays.lean with values in any ordered field

What is checked

  • The Sure Thing theorem, for any finitely additive probability measure on any algebra of events.
  • The main proof of the paper, steps (1) to (5): from the recording's values (the coin at 1/2, the ball at 1/2, the two independent) and the construction (two cells that partition your measure, each with the recording's ratio for heads given the day's waking), heads has 1/3.
  • The model in sets of Appendix B: two measures that meet every condition of the proof, so the theorem is not empty. A four-point case does the same for section 5.3.
  • Section 5.1: the subdivision lemma; (S1), Sure Thing inside E; (S3), which is (B): in any later measure in which E is certain and the cells of E have the ratio for X that they are defined with, the unconditional probability of X is the conditional one; (S4).
  • Section 5.3: Jeffrey's rule (J) is defined as a measure. It gives E the value u and keeps the ratio inside E. Then the conflict: with her reliability at 9/10 and x at p, the measure in which her saying it is certain has x at 9p/(8p + 1); (J) with u = 9/10 has x at 9/10; the two differ unless p = 1/2; and whatever value (J) is given for x, it gives her saying it less than 1. Run from Sunday on R, (J) gives heads 1/2 given R at every u, and no measure has that together with 1/3 in each cell of R.

What the files assume, said plainly

  • Measures are finitely additive, on an algebra of events. In YourProof.lean, Displays.lean and Cases.lean they take rational values. The two files with any_field in the name state the same theorems with values in any ordered field, the real numbers included. There the finite models stay over the rationals, and four statements are left out: three about limits (an ordered field need not be Archimedean) and one footnote's arithmetic with fixed numbers.
  • In (S3), 'with the same definitions' is written as: the later measure gives X, in each cell of E, the ratio the first measure gives it. From that and E certain the value follows. YourProof.lean also states it with the definitions as one object (bayes_of_definitions). That the definitions are the same at the later point is argued in the paper, section 5.1; Lean checks what follows from it.
  • No measure meets the conditions of the main proof and gives heads a value other than 1/3 (no_other_value).

How to run it

  1. Install Lean 4 (https://lean-lang.org). The files were checked with version 4.36.0.
  2. Run each file by itself:
    lean YourProof.lean
lean Displays.lean
lean OneBoxing.lean
lean Cases.lean
lean YourProof_any_field.lean
lean Displays_any_field.lean

Each run should end without any error or warning. It prints, for the results named at the end of the file, the axioms they rest on: propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound, or fewer of these, or none. These are the axioms of Lean's standard logic. No library beyond Lean's own is used. The lines each run should print are in the files expected_output_*.txt.

How to read it

Each theorem carries a comment naming the display or the sentence of the paper that it states. Lean certifies that each statement follows from the hypotheses written in front of it. Whether a statement says what the paper's sentence says is for the reader to check, by reading the two side by side.

The letters

As in the paper: the Greek letter pi is your measure, and Pr is the recording's.

The numbered displays (Displays.lean)

The paper's other numbered displays, each with the justification the paper gives after it: (6) and (7) of section 4; the tag-swapping lemma, (8) to (13), and (14); the measures set against the proved one, (15) to (22); the full measure, (23) and (24); (25); Sure Thing for a finite partition of n cells; (S5); Appendix A, (A1) to (A4); Appendix C, (C1) to (C3). Each theorem's comment gives the display's number, the display as it stands in the source, and any choice made in stating it.

The tag-swap equation by a second partition (in Displays.lean, not a display of the paper)

In the lemma, (13) is derived from the equation pi(H | C) = pi(H | not-C). The file also derives that equation: with a second pair of cells, being certain that today is the day the ball marks and being certain that it is the other day, the ball has the urn's proportion c in each cell; Sure Thing gives pi(C) = c; with the lines of (10) that is A = B = 1/3. From it: (14) at every urn, pi(H | C) = 1/3, and, with C certain, pi(H) = 1/3; and the second line of (22).

The uneven urn has a model (in Displays.lean)

For every proportion c strictly between 0 and 1, the recording's measure on the four outcomes and your measure on the six points (c/3, c/3, (1-c)/3 on the Monday points; (1-c)/3, c/3, (1-c)/3 on the Tuesday points) meet all the conditions of the lemma and of the second partition together, and the general theorems are applied to them. At c = 99/100 the values of (22) are read off the model.

Numbers given in the text (in Displays.lean)

  • After (19): the difference from 1/3 is exactly 2/(3(3N + 4)), and it is below any positive number from some N on.
  • After (21): the calculation with an uneven urn, stated with a measure.
  • Section 5.1, the footnote with the die: 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3 and 1/6 + 1/4 = 5/12.
  • Section 5.3, the footnote: 0.9 if you are sure of what she said, 0.74 if you are 0.8 sure, and, with the same doubt at every level, values that go down toward 1/2: above 1/2 at every level, and below 1/2 + e from some level on, for every positive e.
  • Section 6.1, the gas stations: the manifesto's measure as a finite model (the coin and the three urns, sixteen points). Heads has 1/3 at a meeting at either station. With any proportion in the where urn, heads has 1/3 given a meeting. With one station's balls only in the where urn, two thirds of the meetings are at that station, with heads at 1/2 there and 0 at the other. With a 'meet' ball drawn with chance s, heads has s/(1 + s) at a meeting, at either station, which is 1/2 at s = 1.

Section 4.1 (OneBoxing.lean)

What is stated and accepted:

  • Two boxes is a thousand more whatever the opaque box holds.
  • The arranger: the opaque box holds the million iff you act against what your rule recommends. Then, whatever is recommended, acting against it gets more than acting on it. A rule that takes the arrangement into account goes from one box to two and from two to one: it has no recommendation it can keep.
  • The two-boxer's rule compares the acts with the box as it is. Whatever was recommended and whatever the box holds, it gives two boxes, so it keeps its recommendation.
  • Gideon's paradox: if the prize is won exactly by an irrational choice, and a choice that wins is thereby not irrational, the prize is not won.
  • The mixture of the two rules: with the weight on the one-boxing rule halved again and again, the act settles after one halving at two thousand, two at four thousand, ten at a million (for any weight to start from above 0.512 and at most 1), and for every number of halvings there is a prize at which it has not settled.
  • With the opaque box empty, every chance of taking one box is a loss, and the one chance that loses nothing is 0.

What is not stated, because it is not a calculation: that anyone who gives a reason to one-box follows a rule that takes the arrangement into account, and that the arranger can act on any rule whatever, a coin included. These are argued in the paper, section 4.1.

The cases (Cases.lean)

Where the paper describes a case with chances, the case is built as a finite model, a list of points with weights, and the paper's values are derived on it. Each theorem quotes the sentence it states and names every choice made in stating it.

  • Sections 1 and 2 on the model of Appendix B, and (B2) as sets.
  • Section 3: the two guesses; Jack and Jill, with your measure in each cell equal to the recording's conditional for every event of the experiment; Gilad's room and box, at the even urn and at any urn.
  • Section 4: the worth of the ticket to each side at any price, and EDT's price per run.
  • Section 5: the peanut butter (an event independent of the ratio splits E into two cells with the same ratio); the dice; Amina's recording at 0.9; (J) at u = 0; two items taken in opposite orders, where (J) can give two values and conditioning gives one; the noisy signal and the reliable device; the sum for heads in Amina's case, and its two values at u = 1.
  • Section 6.1: the gas stations with every urn as a parameter: the owner who moves the where urn, the altruist, both urns his and the eighth of all the customers, the whether urn, and the second before you pull in.
  • Section 7: Sleeping Beauty on Sunday at any urn; the measure (15) on the six points; the measure with the four quarters; double halfing; outsiders in the reference class; the Doomsday factor and its limit; the bets of section 7.4; the Monday clock-time prior.
  • Section 8: the pairs of runs at any bias; which pair is the coin; a rule's cell probabilities at an uneven urn; the Wednesday case at every urn, and the 3/7 that a self-sampling rule gives there; the arithmetic of the ur-prior rules; the reference-class case; the lemma's cells at every urn.
  • Section 9: tag-swapped driving (1/4, 1/8, the prize at 1/3 given either sign); Monday balls only and the driver's coin, 1/(1+p); the payoffs at the intersection and of a coin chosen in advance, for the paper's payoffs and for any payoffs; the division that gives the first intersection (2-p)/2; the urn on the clock-times; the planner's two events.
  • Section 10.1: the train at even odds and at any price of the third ticket, and its cell values as the lemma's ratios.

Not stated: two footnotes that appeal to the law of large numbers, a sum over infinitely many cells (the measures here are finitely additive), and sentences that are arguments and not calculations.
